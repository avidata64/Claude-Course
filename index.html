Is the model fixed? Yes, and the product keeps changing

What stays fixed

  • Your chat cannot retrain or break it

What changes

  • Features, limits and menus

Why a bloated chat costs you

Limits

Long chats use up your allowance sooner.

Speed

Replies get slower as the chat grows.

Quality

Early instructions can get lost, and answers can drift.

Clutter

Old tangents and corrections stay in the context.

It is not the number of messages that hurts. It is irrelevant and repeated material carried along every turn.

Five ways to keep your chats lean

1. Summarize, then start fresh

"Summarize our decisions in 10 lines I can paste into a new chat."

2. Three questions in one

Ask related questions together, not in three chats.

3. Edit, do not follow up

Fix the original message instead of stacking corrections.

4. Paste only what you need

Chapter 3, not the whole 40-page PDF.

5. Use a smaller model

Haiku or Sonnet for small jobs, Opus only when needed.

Bonus

Keep stable context in memory, preferences or a Project, so you never re-paste it.

AI drafts. You deliver.

You are accountable for what reaches your students. Talk to it, then fine-tune.

Before you deliver

  • Accurate: facts, numbers, sources
  • Right level: grade, language
  • Fair: bias, inclusion
  • Usable: can I teach this tomorrow?
  • Mine: does it sound like me?

Talk to it

  • Say what is wrong: "too advanced"
  • Ask for changes to one part only
  • Two or three rounds are usually enough
  • If it drifts, summarize and start fresh

Don't follow-up questions bloat too? Yes, and here is the fix

Rich context once, then few precise follow-ups.

1. Front-load context

Audience, goal, constraints and format in one first prompt.

2. Let Claude ask once

"Ask me any clarifying questions first." Answer them all in one reply.

3. Edit the original

Do not stack corrections.

4. Store stable context

Memory, preferences and Projects, so you never re-paste.

Rule to remember: rich first prompt, few precise follow-ups. Then summarize and restart.

Write once, use everywhere: your Context Card

Six lines about you and your teaching. One card that follows you through both days.

Context Card
1. Who I am: role, subject, institution, region
2. Who I teach: level, class size, language, learner needs
3. Standards: curriculum, learning outcomes
4. Voice and format: tone, reading level, structure
5. Quality bar: difficulty mix, question types, rubric style
6. Guardrails: no student data, say when unsure, cite sources

Why a card?

  • Stable context lives outside the chat, so chats stay lean
  • You edit it in one place
  • It becomes preferences, Project instructions, skill rules and prompts

Turn the card into a preferences file

Draft it as a .md file you can edit, then paste it into your preferences.

# My teaching preferences
- I teach [subject] to [grade/level], about [number] students, [language].
- Write in plain, warm English at [reading level].
- Lesson plans: objective, hook, activity, check for understanding, homework.
- Quizzes: a mix of recall, application and one reasoning question,
  always with an answer key.
- Never include real student names. If you are unsure of a fact, say so.
- For a big task, ask up to 3 clarifying questions first.
Where it goes
Settings, Profile, personal preferences. Or a Project's instructions.
Test it
Open a new chat and ask for a short quiz.
Keep it short
One page. Long preferences waste context.

Skills: a recipe card for work you repeat

You repeat tasks too: the same quiz structure, the same feedback, the same parent email.

What it is

Saved instructions for one kind of task. Claude uses them when a request fits.

Where to find it

Customize, then Skills. Create one, or upload a skill folder as a ZIP. Needs code execution turned on in Settings.

How it differs

Preferences are about you. A Project is about one body of work. A skill is about one task.

Pillars to consider: teaching, assessing, assigning, giving feedback, communicating, including every learner.

Demo: create a skill, then call it

---
name: assessment-designer
description: Designs quizzes, tests and rubrics for my courses.
  Use when I ask for a quiz, question bank, test or rubric.

# Assessment designer
Follow my Context Card for level, language and tone.
Inputs: topic, level, learning outcomes, time available.
Output: a blueprint table (outcome, question type, marks), the
questions, an answer key and a marking scheme.

no real student names; say when unsure.

Projects: one workspace for everything you built

A Project holds instructions, knowledge files and its own chats, so new chats start informed but clean.

Instructions

Your Context Card, plus a few project rules.

Knowledge

Lesson plan, quiz bank, skill text, notes. No student data.

Chats

Same context, fresh history. That keeps chats lean.

Keep it small. Free accounts can have up to five Projects, and project files load into the chat.

Connectors: let Claude read your own files

A connector links Claude to an app you already use. Today: Google Drive and one Google Sheet.

What it does

Claude can search and read your Drive files. Sheets are read as plain table text.

What to watch

It sees what you can see. Start with questions, and approve any change.

What it costs

Connectors use up context, so connect only what you need.

The dataset in your Sheet is simulated, so no real student is ever exposed.

Demo: connect Drive and open your Sheet

  1. Open the connectors list and choose Google Drive
  2. Sign in to Google and allow access
  3. In a chat, choose Add from Google Drive, or ask Claude to find the Sheet by name
  4. Ask: "What is in this sheet? Describe the columns and anything odd."
  5. Ask for the row count and check it

Stay safe

  • Use a personal account unless your institution's policy allows otherwise
  • Disconnect when you finish
  • Ask for answers, not changes, for now

Your turn: decide what you want from this data

  1. Write first: three questions of your own, on paper, before asking Claude
  2. Explore: follow your curiosity and note what surprises you
  3. Challenge: ask Claude to argue against your best finding

Think about

Debrief: what did you find, and what did you doubt?

Surprised

Which belief did the data refuse to support?

Doubted

Asked better

Good data work is mostly good questions. Claude can calculate, but you decide what is worth asking.

Claude now marks what it generates

Machine-readable marks, not a visible stamp.

Text

An imperceptible watermark woven into the text. It travels with copy and paste. Supported models only.

Files

Signed provenance data (Content Credentials) on generated files such as images.

Checking

A free Content Checker for files. Text detection is in private preview for eligible organizations, including educational ones.

Marks are a clue, not a verdict.

What this means for student work

It can suggest

That Claude may have processed a piece of text or a file.

It cannot prove

  • Misconduct on its own
  • That no other AI was used
  • Anything once text is reworded

Better evidence

  • Drafts and version history
  • A short oral follow-up
  • Prompts and checking notes handed in
Do not accuse a student on a detector result alone.
